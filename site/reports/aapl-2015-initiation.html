Investment Thesis

Priced for peak iPhone, built for a decade of Services

We recommend initiating a position in Apple. At about $107 the stock trades near 11.6 times trailing earnings, below the S&P 500 multiple and lower still after its net cash. The market is pricing Apple as a hardware maker whose best product has peaked.

We think that view misses three things. The installed base keeps growing, and customers rarely leave the ecosystem. Services revenue grows with that base rather than with new unit sales. And the cash flow is large enough to fund a $200 billion capital return program while still adding to the balance sheet.

Fiscal 2015 was a record year: revenue up 28% to $233.7 billion, 231 million iPhones sold, and Greater China revenue up 84%. Even if fiscal 2016 iPhone units decline, the stock already discounts that outcome.

Catalysts

  1. Services visibilityServices reached about $20 billion in fiscal 2015. As it grows faster than hardware and is disclosed in more detail, we expect investors to value it like a software business.
  2. Capital return updateApple usually expands its buyback and dividend each spring. A larger program would shrink the share count further.
  3. Upgrade cycleHundreds of millions of users are still on iPhones older than the iPhone 6. The fall 2016 iPhone gives them a reason to upgrade.
  4. China and emerging marketsRising incomes in China and India expand the market for premium smartphones.

Risks to the Thesis

  1. iPhone 6s cycleReports of supplier order cuts suggest fiscal 2016 iPhone units could decline from the record year.
  2. China slowdownA weaker Chinese economy and currency could hit Apple's fastest-growing region.
  3. Product concentrationiPhone is about two-thirds of revenue.
  4. Unproven new categoriesApple Watch has not shown it can become a major product line.

Why we are buying Apple

The market's mistake

Investors treat each iPhone cycle as a one-time event and value Apple on the assumption that the next one will be smaller. In reality, each cycle starts from a larger installed base of loyal customers. Apple's revenue is better described as a subscription to the ecosystem, paid every two or three years, than as a series of hit products.

The ecosystem is the asset

iOS, iCloud, iMessage and the App Store tie a user's photos, apps and contacts to Apple devices. Switching to Android means losing purchases and habits. That stickiness lets Apple charge premium prices and earn about 40% gross margins in a hardware business where competitors earn far less.

Services: a second business hiding in plain sight

Services, at about $20 billion, would rank among the 150 largest US companies on its own. It grows with the number of active devices, not with new unit sales, and carries higher margins than hardware. At today's price investors pay almost nothing for it.

Shareholder returns

Apple pays $0.52 a quarter and is midway through a $200 billion capital return program. At this valuation, buybacks retire shares at roughly an 8–9% earnings yield, which mechanically raises earnings per share each year.

What would make us wrong

A shrinking installed base for two consecutive years, a cut to the capital return program, or Services growth falling below growth in active devices. Any of these would mean the ecosystem is weakening, and we would sell.
